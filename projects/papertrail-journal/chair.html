Why one good chair outlives five trends.

A visit to a workshop where repair is designed in from the beginning.

Words by Mara VennAugust 9, 20268 min read
ObjectDining chair
White oak
Hide glue
Replaceable rush seat

The chair on Hannah Bell’s workbench is not new. That is the point. Its front stretcher is polished dark where shoes have touched it for thirty years, and the finish on one arm has gone satin-smooth from use. The seat, however, is fresh. Bell replaced it that morning.

“A chair should have parts you can sacrifice,” she says. “The seat wears out. The joints loosen. The finish gets marked. None of those things should mean the chair is finished.”

Her workshop builds furniture with an unfashionable assumption: that somebody other than the maker will eventually have to open, tighten, reweave, refinish, or replace part of it. Joints are chosen partly for strength and partly for intelligibility. Seats can be removed. Glues can be reversed with heat and moisture. Hardware is ordinary enough to source again.

Durability is not the same as hardness

Furniture advertising often treats durability as a material property: harder wood, thicker steel, denser foam. Bell thinks of it as a system. The strongest component is not helpful if the first weak component cannot be repaired.

She points to two dining chairs waiting near the wall. One is mass-produced, barely five years old, with a cracked molded shell bonded permanently to its frame. The other is from the 1960s and has arrived for its second seat replacement.

“The old chair is not immortal. It is just allowed to fail gracefully.”

That phrase explains much of what separates long-lived objects from merely sturdy ones. Good repairable design expects wear. It decides where wear should happen and makes that place accessible.

The economics of keeping

A handmade chair costs more at the beginning, sometimes much more. The argument for it becomes clearer only across time. One chair that takes three new seats and two rounds of joint repair may serve several generations while five cheaper chairs pass through a room.

There is also the value created by familiarity. The object changes category. It stops being “a chair” and becomes “the chair from the old kitchen,” or “the one your grandfather repaired,” or simply the object everyone reaches for without thinking.

That value cannot be manufactured quickly because time is one of its ingredients.

Designing for the unknown repairer

Before a chair leaves the workshop, Bell writes the wood species, finish, glue, and seat material beneath the frame in pencil. The note is invisible in use. It is meant for a person she may never meet.

“The respectful thing,” she says, “is to assume the object will outlive your involvement with it.”

Then she turns the repaired chair upright, sits in it, shifts her weight, and listens. No creak. No movement. The chair is ready to go home again.
