The last repair shops on a changing street.

Three blocks, seven storefronts, and the people who still know how to fix things instead of replacing them.

Words by Lena OrtizPhotographs by Sam K.August 12, 202611 min read
Field notesWest Mercer Street
Tuesday, 8:10 a.m.
Doors open before the coffee shop.

At 8:17 on a Tuesday morning, before the bakery has put its chalkboard on the sidewalk, a man named Daniel Cho is already leaning over a toaster that is older than the college student who brought it in. The toaster is upside down on a square of brown paper. Its chrome shell has been removed. Inside, a darkened contact is being cleaned with a strip of emery cloth no wider than a shoelace.

“People usually think the heating element is dead,” Cho says. “Half the time it is just the switch.” He presses the lever with his thumb. There is a precise metallic click. “That click is good.”

Cho’s shop, Mercer Electric, has occupied eleven feet of storefront since 1989. The sign above the window is faded blue enamel. Two doors down is a shoe repairer. Across the street, a tailor. At the corner, a watchmaker whose display contains no watches for sale—only handwritten cards listing services: crystal, stem, crown, balance, clean.

A street organized around keeping things

There was a time when every commercial street had some version of this cluster. Repair was not a specialized ethical position. It was simply one of the ordinary things a neighborhood required, like bread, keys, hemming, and a place to have a zipper replaced.

On Mercer, the old logic survives in fragments. A lamp goes to Cho. A boot goes to Rosa Mendez. A torn coat goes to Aram Bedrosian. None of them describe their work in the language of sustainability, though their shops prevent an extraordinary amount of material from becoming waste.

“If someone likes a thing enough to carry it through my door, I assume it is worth trying to save.”

Mendez says this while holding a pair of brown boots whose soles have worn nearly smooth. She runs a thumbnail along the welt and explains that the upper is good, the stitching is sound, and the leather has already done the difficult work of becoming comfortable. The repair will cost less than a comparable new pair, but not dramatically less. That is not why the owner brought them.

“People think repair is about cheap,” she says. “Sometimes it is. But mostly it is about not starting over.”

The knowledge hidden inside old objects

Repairers learn products in a way their designers rarely can. They encounter the same hinge after five years of use, the same seam after ten thousand steps, the same motor after a decade of heat and dust. They see where materials fail and where they refuse to.

Cho keeps a drawer of switches, thermostats, ceramic insulators, screws, knobs, and cords harvested from appliances that could not be saved. Nothing in the drawer is catalogued. He knows its contents spatially. When a cream-colored mixer from the 1970s arrives with a cracked speed-control plate, he opens the third drawer, reaches behind a box of cloth-covered wire, and produces a plate that fits.

This inventory makes less sense every year. New products arrive sealed with adhesive, clipped together in ways designed for assembly rather than disassembly, or dependent on electronic boards that are unavailable as replacement parts. “You can tell when nobody expected a second person to open it,” Cho says.

What disappears when a repair shop closes

The obvious answer is a service. The less obvious answer is a type of local memory. Repair shops know which apartment buildings have odd window hardware, which restaurant still uses a forty-year-old mixer, which school buys chairs with replaceable glides, which brand of winter boot can take a second sole and which cannot.

That knowledge is difficult to quantify because it lives mostly in hands, drawers, and conversations. It is not transferable in a folder when a lease ends.

Mercer Street has lost four repair businesses in six years. Two became apartments. One became a chain eyewear shop. One remains empty behind butcher paper. The surviving shops are busy, but busyness does not guarantee survival. Their work requires space, time, and customers willing to accept that a good repair may take a week.

At 4:40 in the afternoon, Cho plugs in the toaster. The lever catches. The coils glow evenly. He lets it run for thirty seconds, releases it, and unplugs it again.

The invoice will read: clean contacts, adjust latch, test. Twenty-eight dollars.

It is a small transaction. It is also a small refusal to begin again.
